# Research rigor

## Label every finding

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Confirmed | Someone outside the company said or did it, dated, with a source |
| Inferred | Your reasoning from confirmed facts. Say how |
| Assumed | A belief with no evidence yet. Name the cheapest test |
| Unknown | No signal. Absence of evidence is Unknown, never "no" |

Keep a list of what's still assumed, with its test. Nobody on the team counts as evidence.

## When a finding is real

1. **Three channels beat one.** A review, a forum post and a job ad are three channels. Three reviews on one site are one.
2. **A one-channel theme** goes on a watchlist with a review date. It isn't a finding yet.
3. **Use counts with denominators.** Write "9 of 14", never "64%" on a small sample.
4. **Say so when a source is silent:** label it "quiet" or "couldn't reach", not "no complaints".
5. **Never synthesise a quote.** A quote is exact, with its URL, or it isn't a quote.

Trust evidence in this order: our own measured results, our own account data, published benchmarks, expert opinion, intuition. Benchmarks from other companies are a starting point, not a forecast.

## Why people buy

Study people who already switched. Ask or look for what triggered the search, what they tried first, and what almost stopped them. Sort what you find by force:

- **Push:** what's wrong today.
- **Pull:** what attracts them to the new way.
- **Anxiety:** what they fear about changing.
- **Habit:** what keeps them where they are.

Write the job as: "When ___, I want ___, so I can ___."

## The language bank

Keep the exact phrases buyers use: phrase, where it was found, date, speaker's role. Discount sales calls, which echo our own words back. Keep users' words apart from buyers' words. Mark each phrase adopt, bridge or refuse.

## Free competitor sources

Pricing pages, changelogs, job posts, review sites, and the public ad libraries from LinkedIn, Google and Meta.
